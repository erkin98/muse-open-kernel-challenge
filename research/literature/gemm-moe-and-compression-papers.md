# GEMM, MoE and weight-compression sources

This file covers Stream-K, MARLIN, Machete, the PyTorch column-major MoE blog, DFloat11, the fused-Huffman GEMV repo and ZipServ, and what each means for the 4-bit MoE and BF16 projection boards on an H200.

Most of these sources confirmed that our entries already contain the strong ideas: an evict-first weight stream, register-sourced wgmma, bit-field FP4 decode, stream-K with an FP32 reduce, and 12-bit lossless BF16 weights. What was left is small and uncertain.

## Stream-K against what a fused W4A16 MoE kernel needs

The paper gives no direct evidence for skinny, bandwidth-bound shapes. Its corpus is compute-bound GEMM with m, n, k from 128 up, and the authors say Stream-K is noisy or loses in small memory-bound shapes because the fix-up adds memory traffic. What carries over is the structure and two named weaknesses.

How it works. Stream-K splits the total count of small multiply-accumulate loop iterations evenly (within one) over a fixed grid of CTAs. Each CTA's range walks through tiles in m, then n, then k order and may cross tile boundaries. In the basic scheme the CTA that does a tile's first K-iteration owns the output tile. The others write partial sums to temporary global storage and raise a flag, and the owner waits and accumulates them serially. Communication, synchronisation and temporary storage scale with the grid, not with the problem.

Weaknesses the paper names. The simple hybrid of data-parallel plus one-tile stream-K hides latency poorly when three or more CTAs cover a tile, because the owner's serial accumulation then causes imbalance. The shipped hybrid, two-tile stream-K plus data-parallel, drops one full data-parallel wave so each stream-K CTA gets between one and two tiles and an owner receives from only one peer. It needs at least two full data-parallel waves to pay, and it is motivated by cache skew, since CTAs start at different K offsets and lose cross-CTA reuse.

Cost model. The appendix models CTA time as a fixed cost, plus a conditional partial-output cost, plus a per-iteration cost, plus a per-peer accumulate cost, with constants measured once per tile shape, dtype and GPU. The best grid can be smaller than the SM count: a 64-tile square case is best at 64 CTAs on 108 SMs, and a single-tile huge-K case is best at 8 CTAs because the per-peer cost lands on one CTA.

Evaluation. A100 with locked clocks, 32,824 shapes with m, n, k log-sampled between 128 and 8192, FP16 inputs with FP32 accumulate, one 128x128x32 tile. Averages: 1.63x over the CUTLASS data-parallel kernel, 1.13x over cuBLAS, 1.12x over an oracle picking the best CUTLASS tile per shape. Peaks 14.7x, 6.74x and 4.63x. Worst cases 0.80x, 0.64x and 0.61x. The authors suggest a separate cost model or a second smaller-tile kernel for the memory-bound regime, and list Morton-order tile ordering as future work.

What we have. The paper treats one dense GEMM with uniform tiles and says nothing about grouped or ragged GEMM. Our moe kernel handles that with a job table built after routing, a largest-tile-count-first queue order, an atomic claim counter, release and acquire completion counters, FP32 vector `red` adds for split-unit partials, and programmatic dependent launch. For M of 17 and up it already cuts the work into 132 equal-cost ranges (about 8 CTAs per expert at the d32 load, 5 at v32). The per-expert slope is bytes over bandwidth, 28.31 MB / 4.6 TB/s, about 6.15 us. The loss sits at unit boundaries: release fences of about 0.9 us per unit per warpgroup, epilogue and split-unit atomics, and a 3.5 to 6.9 us end-of-phase spread. We ship separate skinny, mid and fat builds, so the suggestion of a second, smaller-tile kernel is already in.

Inference (ours, not stated by either source): imbalance in our kernel is no longer a granularity problem, so the remaining levers are the fix-up cost structure. The measured end-of-phase spread and per-unit fences are the same class of cost as the paper's per-peer and partial-output terms. If our tile table showed three or more CTAs per output tile, a cap on collaborators per tile, chosen from the cost model and dispatched on contiguous M ranges, would be the unused idea.

Gaps. No Stream-K measurement on skinny M or grouped GEMM exists in the paper, and we did not read CUTLASS grouped-GEMM scheduler write-ups. Tiles per CTA and collaborators per tile in our kernel were not measured at the time.

## MARLIN, Machete and the FP4 dequant path

MARLIN keeps 4-bit weights bandwidth-bound up to batch 16 to 32. On an A10, for one very large 72k x 18k matrix with group size 128, it gets about 3.9x over FP16 up to batch 16 to 32 (ideal 3.87x, since scales add overhead) and falls toward 1.5x at batch 128. End-to-end serving speedup over FP16 at batch 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128:

|Setup|1|2|4|8|16|32|64|128|
|-|-|-|-|-|-|-|-|-|
|A10, Llama-2-7B|2.93|3.19|3.02|2.90|2.74|2.26|1.78|1.20|
|A100, Yi-34B, one GPU|2.90|2.88|2.86|2.79|2.69|2.36|1.71|1.18|
|A100, Llama-2-70B, two GPUs|2.55|2.59|2.57|2.53|2.42|2.08|1.52|1.19|

The headline claim is up to 2.8x at batch 16. Speedups are lower on A100 than on an RTX 3090 because the A100's bandwidth and compute make start-up latency and imperfect partitioning relatively larger. At prefill-size batches on A100 it matches an uncompressed compute-bound matmul up to batch 1024 with about 10% slowdown beyond that.

Pipeline and layout, in plain words:

- Output tiles are M x N_sm with N_sm of 64, 128 or 256, so the whole batch shares one read of a weight block.
- Activations are reloaded from L2. Weights are read exactly once with 16-byte loads, so a warp reads 1,024 4-bit weights per instruction.
- Weights are reshuffled offline into 16 x 64 tiles so each thread's 16-byte vector holds the 8 weights it needs for four different 16x16 tensor-core blocks.
- Loads use cp.async with an evict-first hint so streaming weights do not push out activations. Pipeline depth 4 gives fully static shared-memory addresses. Activation tiles use an XOR swizzle for conflict-free ldmatrix.
- Dequantisation ORs bits into the FP16 exponent field and subtracts a magic constant, two values per 32-bit register, with the zero point folded into the same subtract.
- Each warp has a fixed 64-wide sub-tile and the K range is split across warps, reduced in shared memory.
- The partition is striped, similar to Stream-K, and the reduction is serial from the bottom stripe up, done in FP16 in the output buffer so it stays in L2.
- Scale lesson: loading a scale once per two tiles, the theoretical minimum, made the compiler reorder instructions badly and cost 10 to 20% in some shapes. Reloading from shared memory for every sub-tile kept peak speed.

Hopper. The Machete write-up says Marlin struggles on H100 because it uses the old mma instruction, which loses about 37% of Hopper peak compute. Machete uses wgmma, solves the transposed problem so the dequantised weights are the register-sourced operand (no shared-memory round trip), and adds TMA loads and warp specialisation. It wins from batch 128 up. Its stated future work still lists batches under 32, so it does not claim to beat Marlin in the memory-bound range. It pre-shuffles weights so one 128-bit shared load serves a tensor-core operation after interleaving four tiles.

FP4 on Hopper. The serving library's Marlin FP4 (E2M1) dequant moves the sign bit and three code bits of each FP4 value into the sign, exponent and top mantissa of a 16-bit float, two values per 32-bit lane, so the result is the FP4 value times a fixed power of two. The header's design note says zero-point and scale work is folded into the following floating-point step. Community pages we saw only as search snippets say Hopper has no FP4 tensor cores, that the serving library runs NVFP4 checkpoints there through the Marlin weight-only path, and that one benchmark found INT4 on Marlin 9 to 13% faster than NVFP4 on Marlin on 2 x H200 (https://huggingface.co/canada-quant/GLM-5.2-W4A16-MTP/discussions/2, https://docs.vllm.ai/en/v0.10.2/api/vllm/model_executor/layers/quantization/utils/marlin_utils_fp4.html). We treat that as unverified.

What our moe entry already has, from reading its source: mma.sync for the skinny unit (16 rows, warps splitting K) and register-sourced wgmma for the fat band; weights through cp.async.bulk with mbarriers and an L2 evict-first policy; intermediates evict-last; a 32-bit word of 8 E2M1 codes turned into four bf16x2 fragment registers carrying a fixed power-of-two factor; and E4M3 scale decode either from a shared-memory table or by one byte-permute plus one integer multiply, checked in prepare(). So the FP4 field placement is already a multiply-based decode, and there is nothing new to import from that path except confirming the details match.

Two inferences. First, the K-permutation that decodes each scale pair once per four k16 steps (the scale-sharing layout other teams call KPERM3) is the kind of irregular reload pattern MARLIN found fragile against compiler instruction ordering. If a check on it disappoints, inspect the SASS ordering around the main loop before blaming the idea. Second, MARLIN reduces partials in FP16 in an L2-resident buffer, while our entry uses FP32 vector red adds. The rules now allow BF16 or FP16 split partials, so a half-precision partial format is a possible traffic cut. It is untested, and it touches the replay hazard described in cuda-docs-and-replay-hazards.md.

For tokens 1 to 32 the memory-bound analysis holds on any GPU generation. For 128 to 256, Machete says the wgmma path is required, which both of our entries have for their wide routes.

## The PyTorch column-major MoE blog

What it claims. The blog is about the serving library's Triton fused-MoE kernel for Mixtral 8x7B in FP16 on A100 and H100 at decode-size batches (https://pytorch.org/blog/accelerating-moe-model/). Two optimisations: SplitK work decomposition (parallelism from M*N tiles to M*N*SplitK, atomics to combine, about 18 to 20% over data-parallel), and column-major scheduling (output tiles ordered so all M-tiles of one weight column run consecutively and the column stays in L2). Headline: up to 4x on A100 and 4.4x on H100. At M=2 on H100 the Nsight numbers are L2 hit rate +254%, global loads -49%, 2.7x faster; 4.4x at batch 512. Stated limits: Triton atomic add has no BF16 so it ran in FP16, launch latency needs CUDA graphs, some expert-mapping inconsistencies appeared end to end, and the next step was a CUDA kernel with fused W4A16 dequant.

What the repo shows (meta-pytorch/applied-ai, kernels/triton/inference/col_major_moe_gemm, contributed by IBM Research). The variants are v0 (grouped), v1 (SplitK) and v2 (column-major). They are separate variants, not stacked, so the roughly 4x is column-major against the other schedules and not SplitK plus column-major. The benchmark shapes are 8 experts, top-2, intermediate 7,168 per half (up-projection width 14,336), hidden 4,096, FP16, M from 1 to 512, with block 16 x 32 x 64 when the token-expert pair count is at most the expert count and 64 x 64 x 32 otherwise. Each M-block is a slice of the expert-sorted token list padded per expert to the block height, so an M-block never mixes experts.

Why it does not pay for us (inference from the sources' shapes). Tokens per local expert average M x 8 / 256, which is M/32: 8 at 256 tokens and below 1 up to M=32. With blocks of 16 or more rows nearly every active expert has exactly one M-block, so column-major order has nothing to reuse. It needs more than one block's worth of tokens on the same expert, as in Mixtral at M=512 with 128 tokens per expert against a 64-row block. This closed the idea by tile-count argument. The confirming check would be to count M-blocks per expert in our job table at M=128 and 256 under skewed routing.

A suspicious helper (our reading of the source, not executed). The v2 column-major index function takes the modulo by the N-tile count and divides by the M-tile count, instead of using the M-tile count for both. Those differ in every benchmark shape. Worked example at the repo's M=2 config: 124 padded rows over 16-row blocks give 8 M-tiles, 14,336 columns over 32 give 448 N-tiles, so 3,584 programs. At most 4 M-blocks are real (4 pairs padded to 16 each), and by our arithmetic only 8 distinct N-tiles out of 448 (about 2% of the output width) would be touched. If that is right, the small-M speedups partly reflect skipped work, which would also fit the blog's remark about expert-mapping inconsistencies. At M=2 the blog reports 2.7x even though each expert has at most one M-block, so reuse across M-blocks cannot explain it. The repo's own test imports a module that is not in the directory, so we could not confirm that any correctness test passes. Anyone with a GPU can settle this in a few minutes by comparing against a reference.

A post claiming a leaderboard jump from rank 175 to 80 was attached to this blog. We could not identify the poster, and we do not know whether the jump came from the blog or from an unrelated effect.

## DFloat11, the Huffman GEMV repo and ZipServ

DFloat11 does not fuse decode into the matmul. It Huffman-decodes the exponent stream into a full BF16 buffer in HBM and then runs a normal GEMM, so it adds traffic instead of cutting it. At batch 1 inference is about 2x slower than BF16 (README), amortised at larger batch.

- Mechanism: Huffman-code only the 8-bit exponent (entropy about 2.6 bits, only about 40 of 256 exponent values occur), keep sign and mantissa raw in a second array. Maximum code length is capped at 32 bits. The single 2^32-entry table is split into four 256-entry byte tables plus a code-length table, 1,280 bytes in shared memory. A 5-bit-per-thread gap array gives each thread its first valid bit offset. The kernel runs two phases (count outputs per thread, block-wide prefix sum for write positions, then re-decode and write), 8 bytes per thread, 256 threads per block, and all matrices of a transformer block are decompressed together before it runs.
- Compression is 68 to 72% of original size, 10.87 to 11.49 bits per weight across 15 models, bit-exact.
- Headline numbers depend on version. The v1 abstract says 1.9 to 38.8x higher throughput than CPU offload, decompression up to 24.87x faster than CPU-to-GPU transfer and 15.12x faster than nvCOMP. The current HTML says 2.3 to 46.2x, 34.95x and 20.97x. The baselines are CPU offload and nvCOMP, never a fused BF16 kernel. The v0.2.0 release made the kernel 20 to 40% faster.
- Reddit thread, from the authors (all 11 pages read; pages 8 to 11 are only related-thread titles). BF16 exponents carry about 2.6 bits of information, so Huffman-code the exponent for about 11 bits per weight. On an A100 at batch 128 it is "basically just as fast" as BF16 (1.02x difference); at batch 1 about 40% slower (2k input, 100 output tokens). The memory table is about capacity: Llama-3.3-70B on one H200 needs 141.11 GB in BF16 and does not fit, against 96.14 GB for DF11. The authors say 8-bit lossy quantisation is faster and smaller but not lossless, and point to Deep Compression (2016) and ZipNN as prior art for storage-only exponent compression. The thread has no kernel-level answers. It confirms our verdict: their best case at batch 128 is parity, not a speedup.

lszxb/bf16_huffman_infer fuses decompression into a CUDA-core GEMV (templated on batch size, FP32 accumulators, warp reduction, last block writes the output, no tensor cores) for batch up to 8 and falls back to decompress-then-GEMM above that. It claims 80 to 90% of original decode speed and about 25% less VRAM. For 256 tokens at batch 1 with CUDA graphs: Qwen2.5-7B 14.98 s raw against 13.02 s compressed on an RTX 4060 Ti, but 6.66 s against 7.23 s on an RTX A6000; Qwen3-8B 7.75 s against 8.24 s on the A6000. Our reading: the faster the memory relative to the ALUs, the less variable-length decode pays. (The card bandwidths are from our memory, not fetched.)

ZipServ (2603.17435). A fixed-length 3-bit code per weight: seven exponent values that are numerically contiguous in 99.6% of 3,875 matrices, plus a fallback code for outliers stored in full 16 bits. Each 8x8 tile is three 64-bit bitmaps plus two compact value buffers. Average about 11.3 bits per weight against a lower bound of about 10.6. Decode uses bitwise ops, popcount and a 7-entry table, and the profile is full of logic, integer add and popcount instructions. The fused decompress-GEMM is used only in the memory-bound decode phase. Results against cuBLAS: average 1.31x (peak 1.71x) on RTX 4090 and 1.36x (peak 2.21x) on L40S, some small layers 0.79x; 1.34x and 1.87x on RTX 5090 at batch 32. DFloat11 in the same comparison averages 0.28x and 0.34x. On A100 and H800 the authors say it may not always match the cuBLAS baseline, which they attribute to abundant HBM bandwidth relative to compute. End to end it is 1.22x over a production serving stack.

For our decoder we looked at the per-element work. At 96% coverage the average is 11.3 bits (3-bit code, plus 8 bits for covered elements, plus 16 for fallbacks), so at most 5.8% fewer bytes than our 12-bit pack. Per element it needs a popcount over the bitmap for a rank, a data-dependent load from the high-frequency or fallback stream, then exponent = base + code and a pack. That is popcount plus unstructured loads, heavier than our fixed-position 12-bit decode in the m <= 32 regime where the ALU and issue budget is the limit.

Our bf16 format, for comparison: low byte raw (exponent bit 0 plus 7 mantissa bits) plus a 4-bit code for the high byte (sign plus an offset into a per-matrix window of 8 exponent pairs). The kernel rebuilds exact BF16 bits with a few integer ops (lop3, byte permute, one packed add of the window base). Weights outside the window get an exact FP32 correction, and the packing is rejected if more than 1 in 200 escape. Routes: a swapped-operand kernel with no cross-CTA reduction for m up to 32, and TMA plus wgmma GEMMs for m 33 to 256. The entropy of the weights is about 10.46 bits per weight and the 12-bit form gives about 0.7 to 1.0e-4 escapes. The byte headroom left under 12 bits is therefore bounded: 5.8% at 11.3 bits, 12.8% at the entropy floor, and only in the memory-bound range.

The H200 has more bandwidth than the A100 and H800 that ZipServ tried (spec figures from memory, not fetched; our local docs use 4.6 TB/s measured), so we expect the extra integer work to eat the saving at least as badly there. That is inference.

## Ideas these sources left, ranked at the time

|Rank|Idea|Board|Status|Why it might pay|
|-|-|-|-|-|

|5|Evict-first hint on the small-m weight stream|bf16, m up to 32|Checked, left as is|The bf16 file's L2 policies apply only to the TMA boxes of the m >= 33 routes, and the bulk-copy helper for the small-m ring carries no hint. Already in an entry or closed, so not to be re-proposed: column-major or SplitK MoE ordering (closed by the one-M-tile-per-expert argument; stream-K already gives SplitK), evict-first weights, register-sourced wgmma, field-placement FP4 decode, E4M3 scale decode by byte permute, the bulk-async ring, 12-bit lossless weights for small m, the no-reduction swapped kernel, TMA wgmma for 33 to 256, Huffman, DFloat11 and rANS (in-register decode loses at m >= 128 because integer ALU work does not overlap the tensor-core GEMM), and Machete as a drop-in (it targets batch 128 and up).

The only magnitudes in this area with a source: the scale-sharing K-permutation at about +1.25% overall per alygg77, and fence cuts at +0.23%. Everything else above is judgment, not measurement.

## What we could not find

- The meaning of a claimed MULDEC graft for MoE (see social-post-claims.md). Multiply-based decode is the only reading consistent with the name, and nothing confirms it.
- Per-batch Machete numbers on H100 (images only) and any MARLIN measurement on Hopper or on block-scaled FP4.
- Whether the bf16 12-bit form could shed its 8-value window to a narrower one at higher escape cost. No source covers it.
